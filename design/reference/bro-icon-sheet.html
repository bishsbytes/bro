bro. identity

The full stop is the accent.

Lowercase: yes, without hesitation. The full stop: yes, but only because it can do a job no other character can — it is the one mark that carries the colour the user chose. Punctuation as a decorative flourish would be a tic. Punctuation as the hook for the personalisation system is an idea.

Change the accent in the top bar. The letterforms are drawn from the same geometry as the interface — 24-unit strokes, circular bowls, round caps — so the mark is built rather than typeset, and it holds at 28px where a set typeface would fill in.

Your two questions

Answered plainly

Lowercase

Yes. BRO in capitals is a shout, and it puts you in the supplement aisle next to the pre-workout tubs. The entire premise of this product is a level voice — an app that describes rather than motivates. Lowercase is that voice rendered as a letterform. It also reads the way the word is actually used between men: quietly, in a text message, at eleven at night.

Hold it in lowercase everywhere, including at the start of a sentence and in the App Store listing. A name that switches case depending on position isn't a name, it's a suggestion.

The full stop

Yes in the logotype, no in the name. A trailing full stop is a common startup tic and on its own it means nothing. Here it earns its place by becoming the accent — the single element that takes the user's chosen hue. The mark is otherwise entirely monochrome, so the dot is the whole of the colour, and a person who picks brass sees their own app icon.

But register the product as bro, unpunctuated. Store search, URLs, support emails and legal filings all handle punctuation badly, and you don't want people typing a full stop to find you. The dot is a graphic device, not part of the name.

Three marks

One recommendation

Recommended

A — full wordmark

Three short letters survive the shrink, so there's no reason to abstract the name away. The dot sits on the baseline at the far right, which is exactly where the eye finishes — the colour is the last thing you read.

Secondary

B — b. monogram

Bigger and calmer, but it gives up the name, and "b." belongs to nobody in particular. Keep it for the favicon, the watch complication, and the notification glyph where the canvas is round and tiny.

Marketing only

C — the dot as a marker

The full stop drops off the baseline and becomes the gauge marker from the product, sitting inside its band. It says what the app does in one move. Too fine for an icon — reserve it for the launch screen, the website header, and the App Store hero.

Required variants

Monochrome

iOS tinted mode and Android monochrome strip your colour, so the dot must still read as a distinct element by value alone — it sits lighter than the letters, not the same weight.

On a home screen

Where every icon competes

Almost every health app on a phone is a saturated gradient with a white glyph. A bone-grey square with three black letters is the only quiet thing on the screen, which is how it gets noticed. This is the one place where being muted is a competitive act rather than a preference.

Worth saying out loud

About the name itself

There is a real tension between calling it bro and building an interface whose first principle is "a quiet instrument, not a coach". That tension can be the best thing about the product — the name is how a man gets his mate to download it, the interface is what keeps him using it after week three. But it only works if the name is the only loud thing. The moment the copy starts saying "let's go" the joke collapses and it becomes the app it was parodying.

Two practical checks before you commit. Trademark: "bro" is a dictionary word, which makes a registration hard to defend and may already be claimed in your class — worth a search before the icon goes anywhere near a store. Audience: the name reads very differently at 24 and at 52. If you want men in their forties and fifties, put it in front of them before you spend another week on it.
