bro. intake proposal

Rough numbers, honestly held.

MyFitnessPal loses men three ways: judgement (red numbers, budgets, streaks), friction (searching four million junk entries to log a sandwich), and fake precision (1,487 kcal presented as truth). bro wins by inverting all three — a personal library that makes logging one tap, totals stated as honest ranges against your own usual, and the three substances every other tracker ignores treated as first-class facts: alcohol, caffeine, and smoking.

The five bets

What "miles better" actually means

1 — Your library beats their database

A man eats and drinks the same ~40 things for 90% of his logging. bro is built around his library, not a global database: recents are one tap, portions remember themselves, and the search field is the fallback, not the front door.

2 — Rough is honest, precise is fake

Nobody weighs a pub lunch. bro accepts "about 600 kcal" as a legitimate entry and carries the uncertainty through: the day's total renders as a soft range on the gauge, not a false-precision integer. No other tracker tells the truth about what it knows.

3 — Usual, not goal

Every total reads against the user's own usual band, exactly like sleep and heart rate. No budget, no remaining, no red. "Roughly 1,850 so far; your days usually land 2,100–2,600" is information. "613 remaining" is a verdict.

4 — Substances are first-class

Alcohol in units, caffeine in milligrams with timing, cigarettes as counts with timing and cost. MFP treats these as awkward foods; for many men they're the whole reason to track. And they're what connects intake to the rest of bro — sleep, mood, heart rate.

5 — Intake earns its keep by connecting

The payoff for logging isn't a pie chart, it's pattern: "On days with three or more drinks, your sleep has averaged 55 minutes shorter." Observed, past-tense, never prescriptive. This is the moat — MFP has no sleep, no mood, no wheel to connect to.

Never — the coupling that hurts people

bro never shows "calories remaining", never suggests compensating, and never couples intake to exercise as earning or burning off. That coupling is MFP's core mechanic and it is exactly the arithmetic of disordered eating. Refusing it is a feature.

Notes on the current build

From your screenshots — quick wins

1
Rows, not stacked cards. "Logged today" and "Browse" render each entry as its own bordered card; the system puts list items in one panel as hairline rows. Halves the visual weight instantly.
2
Group repeats. Two separate "Lager, 1 × pint, 18:17" cards should be one row: "Lager, 4.5% — 2 × pint · 18:17". Logging the same thing twice must never look like a data error.
3
Drop the FAB. It duplicates "Log something" and overlaps the library card. One clear primary action per screen.
4
Human dates. "2026-09-03" is a machine format; the header should read "Thursday 3 September" with the ISO form nowhere in UI.
5
Keep what's right. "Totals are stated without targets, allowances, or ratings", the free-text portion field, and "Enter at least one value" are all exactly on-system — carry them into the new screens untouched.
6
New-item form gains a unit picker. Alongside portion: per-100g / per-portion toggle, and for drinks an ABV field so units compute themselves. Still nothing required beyond a name.

Lines that don't move

Where trackers do harm

No remaining, no earning

Never "calories remaining", never "you've earned", never exercise offsetting intake, never a suggestion to compensate for what was logged. If a user asks for a hard daily limit, that's a target — and targets stay out of bro.

Guidelines are context, opt-in

NHS unit guidance and reference intakes live behind the same setting as population ranges, rendered as a caption, never a coloured zone. A sustained pattern that's clinically meaningful is the alert component's job — once, factual, with a route to a GP.

Rough entries stay respectable

The app never nags a rough entry into a precise one. "You can tighten it later or never" is a promise: precision is offered, never demanded. Demanding precision is how tracking tips into obsession.

Pattern lines are observations

Cross-domain lines always read past-tense and owned by the data — "has averaged", "has tended to" — and never convert into advice. The user draws the conclusion; the app holds the ledger.
